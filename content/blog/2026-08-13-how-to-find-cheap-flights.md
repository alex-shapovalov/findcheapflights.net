Most “cheap flight” tools ask you to pick a destination, a weekend, and two airports you already know. That is a timetable. The cheap inventory is usually sitting on a different day, out of a different airport, or in a city you had not named yet.

A better way to find cheap flights is to hunt: keep the destination loose, expand the origins, and search a real date window against live public fares. The longer version of that loop is [how to find cheap flights with a flexible search](/blog/flexible-search-cheap-flights).

## Start from airports you can actually reach

Your home airport is not the only door. Airports within a few hours — a cheap bus, a short hop, a drive — often price independently. A New York search that only uses JFK will miss EWR and PHL deals. A London search that only uses LHR will miss STN, LGW, and LTN.

On [findcheapflights.ai](/) you pick the airports near you, then optionally expand to others within about six hours. The hunt reads all of them.

## Do not name the city if you do not have to

If the trip is “Europe in March” or “somewhere warm for a week,” locking Paris on day one throws away the cheap city. Flexible-destination mode takes your origins and a continent. The model looks for the stupid-cheap fare in that set, not the cheapest Tuesday to the one airport you typed.

Lock a destination when you already want that city. Then flip the flexibility to origins instead — same idea, other axis.

## Search a window, not a weekend

Tuesday–Wednesday departures are often cheaper than Friday. Shoulder weeks beat school holidays. Schedules that opened 6–18 months out can undercut next month.

Set a date window, or leave dates open and let the hunt sample across the next year or two. For a round trip, give a stay length (for example 3–10 nights) so the return is part of the same fare, not a separate guess. The longer walkthrough is [how to plan around price with flexible date flight search](/blog/flexible-date-cheap-flights).

## Put a ceiling on the fare

A max price tells the hunt what “cheap” means for this trip. A min price is useful too — it filters junk or error fares that are not actually bookable.

If nothing sits inside the band, you still want the closest live fares, marked as over the cap. An empty report is worse than an honest “the floor is $40 above what you asked.”

## Theme the trip so you would take it

A $280 fare to a city with nothing you care about is not a deal. Themes — clubs, beaches, ski, food, Christmas markets — bias the hunt toward places that match the trip, not only the lowest number.

## Read live public fares, then book it yourself

We do not sit on a GDS and we do not ticket the flight. A deal-seeking model searches live public fare pages and writes a report with the listing. You book on the airline or OTA.
