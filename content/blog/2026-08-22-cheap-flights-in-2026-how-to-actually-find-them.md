In 2026, it’s not quite that simple. While it’s true that there are more flight search options available today, finding genuinely cheap flights ultimately boils down to:

- knowing how airfare pricing works
- using the right tools, in the right order
- being flexible in the ways that matter

In this post, we’ll go over everything you need to know about searching for cheap flights. ## The “flight first” rule: start with price, not dates

It’s natural to plan a trip by thinking about dates, but most people plan their trips in the wrong order. Many book hotel rooms and rental cars before they search for flights. If you’re planning a cruise vacation, you’ve likely already booked your itinerary, and now you need to find the best flight to get you there.

Once you fixate on exact dates, you’ve given up bargaining power to the airlines, and the chances of finding cheap flights decreases dramatically. Instead, I recommend beginning with airfare, and letting prices influence your schedule.

1. Do a flights search with your origin and approximate travel window (not exact dates).
2. Check price trends by month/week.
3. See if changing your destination or timing helps, based on the best flights you found.

With tools like Google Flights and findcheapflights.ai, you can easily search for flights from your home airport “to anywhere,” browse an entire month of prices, and find which destinations and dates offer consistently low fares. Once you’ve found your best flights and decided on a schedule, then you can confidently book hotel rooms and activities.

That inversion is the same idea behind a [flexible search](/blog/flexible-search-cheap-flights) and a [flexible date search](/blog/flexible-date-cheap-flights): keep the trip loose until the fare is real.

## When to book: the real “Goldilocks window”

You may have seen blog posts that talk about the “best day of the week to book flights” (usually Tuesday). Airfare algorithms are too complex for any simple day of the week to reign supreme. What does affect price is how many weeks out you book.

Based on aggregated data from several major flight search engines and online travel agencies (OTAs), here are some general guidelines:

Domestic flights tend to be cheapest around 3-4 weeks in advance, although both very early and very last-minute bookings usually end up costing more.

International flights fall into two camps. For “normal” travel periods, most data sets indicate a 1-2 month sweet spot for airfare. For peak summer months, Christmas, or special events, however, finding discounts means booking significantly earlier, typically 3-6 months in advance, or even further for long-haul flights.

A good starting point for price monitoring is at least a month before departure for domestic flights, and two to three months before for international flights. Set up price alerts (email or app-based) so you receive a notification whenever a fare drops, rather than constantly checking the search results page.

## The cheapest day to fly, not to book

The "cheapest day to book" is mostly a myth. It's a much better idea to focus on the cheapest days to fly. Studies have shown that midweek flights are generally cheaper than flights on weekends, especially for routes that are heavily utilized by leisure travelers. This translates to:

- Flight departures on Monday through Thursday are typically less expensive than Friday or Saturday.
- Flights returning midweek are also typically cheaper than a Sunday return.

When searching for flights, use the calendar or whole-month view so you can compare midweek prices against Friday and Sunday at a glance, instead of searching one weekend at a time.

## Use the right tools, in the right order

You do not need every flight site. You need a sequence.

1. Start broad. Use Google Flights Explore, Skyscanner’s “Everywhere,” or [findcheapflights.ai](/) when the city, dates, or even the vibe is still open. Let the cheap inventory show up before you name the trip.
2. Tighten the search. Once you have a destination and a date cluster, compare Google Flights with a metasearch engine like [Skyscanner](https://www.skyscanner.com/). Budget carriers sometimes show up on one and not the other.
3. Confirm on the airline site. The live fare you will actually buy is often a click through to the carrier. Check bags, seats, and change rules before you treat the headline price as the real price.

## Be flexible in the ways that matter

Flexibility is not “I’ll go anywhere, anytime.” It is picking the axes that actually move the fare:

- Destination: search a region or “anywhere” before you lock Paris.
- Airports: include the ones you can actually reach, not only the closest IATA code.
- Dates: shift a few days, or search a month, instead of Friday after work and Sunday night.

You cannot outsmart the pricing algorithm on a frozen itinerary. You can give it more itineraries to choose from. That is the part that still works in 2026, after the Tuesday-booking myth and the extra search tabs have been accounted for.
