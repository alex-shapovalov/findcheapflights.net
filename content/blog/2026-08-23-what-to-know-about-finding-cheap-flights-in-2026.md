But today, airlines update their prices multiple times per day, [Google Flights](https://www.google.com/travel/flights) controls the search results page, and most websites tout themselves as “the best place to find cheap flights.” Fortunately, knowing how prices fluctuate and how to look for flights effectively can help you save significantly compared to other passengers seated next to you. Here’s a framework for looking for flights on the internet right now, with an emphasis on deals rather than clickbait, and how [findcheapflights.ai](/) could fit into that process.

## Why airlines set prices the way they do (and why it matters)

You’ll have better luck finding truly cheap flights if you work with airline pricing instead of against it. Airfare is dynamic. It fluctuates based on route demand, the number of competitors on a given route, fuel costs, season, and even certain days of the week. This explains why a single New York to London flight might cost between $450 and $800 at various points in the same month. Two things follow from this reality: First, there is no such thing as the cheapest day to book. The popular advice to always buy on a Tuesday is obsolete. Instead, the date of your flight and when you make the reservation are more important. Second, the cheapest available flight might not show up on every website or search engine. Metasearch engines, airline websites, and online travel agencies (OTAs) all access different databases and charge different fees. You’ll need a more organized approach than opening a single browser tab and crossing your fingers.

## Finding the sweet spot for booking cheap flights

Studies conducted by outlets like National Geographic and large search engines suggest there is a sweet spot for booking cheap flights. As a general guideline:

- Domestic flights: Low-season travel: 1-3 months prior to departure; High-season (summer, major holidays, etc.): 3-7 months prior
- International flights: Low-season: 2-8 months prior to departure; High-season: 3.5-9 months prior

These aren’t exact science, but they tell you when you should start searching and making flight alerts. If you are well before or after this range, it makes sense to track prices but don’t panic-book. As you reach the end of this time frame and see a great deal (and historical data confirms it’s below normal rates), you’ll usually be safer booking than waiting to see if the price drops further.

## How to use flexibility to find cheap flights

If someone tells me they are having trouble finding cheap flights, they are usually looking for very specific trips (same dates, same airport, maybe same airline). This is the exact moment when you have to deal with whatever fare is available.

The key to finding cheap flights is flexibility across these three areas. Pair this with a [flexible search](/blog/flexible-search-cheap-flights) and a [flexible date search](/blog/flexible-date-cheap-flights) if the city or weekend is not locked.

### Dates

Even a one-day change in departure or arrival dates can mean a big price difference. Flights departing on a Tuesday or Wednesday are generally cheaper than those on a Friday or Sunday. Itineraries during a week in April will often be less expensive than during a holiday week, while mid-summer is typically the most expensive period.

### Airports

Secondary airports and cities close to your destination are worth considering. Flying from Oakland rather than San Francisco, or arriving in Milan and taking a train from there rather than landing directly in Zurich, could result in significant savings. You should be able to include nearby airports as both an origin and a destination, and get results based on a combination of options that you would not want to think through manually.

### Routing

Flying on two separate tickets, using an open-jaw ticket (one way in, one way out), or using a positioning ticket to a larger airport to reach your destination could all prove cheaper than a traditional round-trip. The kinds of itineraries that you can build up creatively using a good flight search engine are the most cost-effective ones, because constructing these by hand is extremely difficult.

## A simple framework for your search

Rather than spending your time jumping between different browser tabs and hoping you have found the best deal, try following the same set of steps every time you search for flights online.

### Step one: get an overview of your options

Use a neutral flight search engine that does not require you to purchase from it. This is a quick pass to see what prices look like in general on your route, and how much of an impact different dates make.

Flexible date views (such as calendars or graphs) are useful for understanding which days in your travel month have the lowest fares. See whether using alternative airports makes a significant difference. In this phase, do not focus on the absolute lowest price you can find, but look for trends.

### Step two: apply stricter filters

Now that you have narrowed down the most promising dates and airports, you should use a more advanced search engine that offers transparent filters and searches a wider range of airlines, including budget and regional carriers where relevant. At this stage, tools such as [findcheapflights.ai](/) can assist you to view as many options as possible, while filtering out any options that seem cheap for the wrong reasons (such as requiring overnight layovers or multiple long detours, or landing at airports far from your final destination).

Things to watch out for:

- Total journey time compared to cost. Dropping $40 from your total flight price may not be worth it if it adds 10 hours and two extra stops to your journey.
- Quality of connections. The short layovers between unallied carriers may appear to be inexpensive, but they are extremely risky in the event of any delays.
- Fare category. A basic economy fare can work well for a brief journey if you really don’t carry luggage, but it is not a bargain when you will need to pay for checked luggage, an assigned seat and, perhaps, a flight change later.

### Pass 3: deciding where to purchase tickets

At this point, you’ll have a handful of flight itineraries (one to three, at most) that look really good. The next question to answer is, where should you actually buy these tickets? From the airline directly? Or via an online travel agency (OTA)?

This is where the distinction between “cheap flights” and “cheap flight bookings” becomes clearer.

## Cheap flights vs buying from the airline

Data from European and UK-based consumer advocacy organisations consistently show the same thing:

- Many OTAs advertise flights that are a few dollars less than the airline’s own price.
- However, after baggage fees and seat assignments are added, the OTA can easily end up being much more expensive, in some cases by over €100 per passenger.
- In some instances, an OTA charges significantly more for luggage and seat selection than the airline does, with the cost hidden somewhere in the checkout process.

So what is the solution here?

Use fare aggregators and comparison engines to research your options, but choose the airline’s own website for purchasing your tickets unless the all-inclusive cost of the ticket (airfare + bags + seat selection) on an OTA is undeniably less and the OTA has an excellent reputation.

When making these comparisons, be sure to look at:

- The final cost, as displayed immediately prior to entering payment information. Discounted rates shown on the initial results page are not necessarily indicative of the final cost.
- Luggage and seat pricing displayed on the OTA, compared with that on the airline’s website. If the OTA is charging more than the airline for bags or seats, you’re being charged a surcharge.
- Ticket change and cancellation policies. Third-party agents may impose their own additional conditions on top of the airline’s policies. Should you need to alter your travel plans, those conditions could turn your ‘cheap’ ticket into an expensive one.

The key to finding the cheapest airfare is to look beyond the headline price, and consider having full control. If you do experience travel disruptions, it will generally be easier to resolve issues directly with the airline rather than dealing with a third party.

## Monitoring prices and automating smartly

Checking the same search results repeatedly is not a great way to find the best price. Today, flight price trackers and alert systems are widely available. That said, they need to be used with a plan in mind.

### When to use price trackers

Use price trackers for:

- Flights you definitely want to take, but don’t yet have specific dates for booked (e.g., “I want to travel to Europe sometime in May”).
- Flights within the Goldilocks pricing zone, in which a discount of 10–20 percent would prompt you to purchase immediately.
- Long-haul flights where $150–$300 price swings are common.

You can mix and match these approaches. For example, plug those constraints into an AI tool like [findcheapflights.ai](/), specifically non-stop or one stop, under $600, travel 7–10 days in March, and let the AI monitor multiple airlines and departure dates.

### What AI does well, and doesn’t

There are two ways that AI has improved flight search:

1. It lets you search by intent rather than specific requirements: “A week somewhere sunny in March, out of Chicago, for less than $500, no red-eyes” instead of city pairs and dates.
2. It can summarize price history into a simple statement: “Prices are currently low for these dates; they are probably not going to get any lower.”

But there are clear boundaries:

- Text-based AI tools that don’t talk directly to live fares can hallucinate fake routes and prices. Never take an AI-generated “deal” at face value. Click through to a real flight search with live inventory before you book.
- Predictions aren’t promises. A recommendation of “Buy now!” or “Wait for a better deal!” might be helpful, but it’s not a guarantee.

Practically speaking, AI is best used as a way to narrow your search, suggest routes or dates you didn’t consider, and tell you whether today’s price is a good deal, bad deal, or average for the route. You should always verify the result in a flight search tool with a live feed from airlines and booking sites.

## Advanced cheap flight strategies (and their tradeoffs)

If you’ve got the basics down, there are a couple more strategies that can sometimes beat standard searches. But each has its drawbacks. The nearby-airport version of this is also in [cheap flights without locking a city](/blog/how-to-find-cheap-flights).

### Positioning flights

It can sometimes be cheaper to fly from your home airport to a big hub on a separate ticket, then from that hub to your final destination. That is especially true for long-haul international trips originating from smaller cities.

Trade-offs: If the first flight is delayed and you miss the connection, the airline operating the second leg has no responsibility to rebook you, since you bought separate tickets. Make sure you have plenty of time between connections, and don’t check luggage if you go this route.

### Open-jaw and multi-city tickets

Buying a one-way ticket to one city and a one-way ticket out of another city can sometimes be cheaper than buying two separate one-way tickets, particularly in places like Europe and Asia where you can easily move between cities. Sometimes multi-city searches also yield unusual routing deals that would otherwise be missed.

Trade-offs: Many flight search engines still don’t handle complex multi-city routing very well, so you’ll likely have to test a few different combinations to make sure you’re getting the best deal.

### Hidden-city and throwaway ticketing

Some people do use tickets that are slightly cheaper because they include a connecting flight in the city you’re trying to reach, but you don’t take the final segment. Specialist websites exist for doing just this. But it is against the terms and conditions of many airlines, so there is always the risk of forfeiting miles, having the rest of your journey cancelled, or running into problems on subsequent reservations. If you’re attached to a certain airline’s frequent flyer scheme, I wouldn’t advise this approach.

## Don’t lose the deal at checkout

Once you’ve located a cheap flight, the next step is making sure your “deal” doesn’t end up being anything like as cheap by the time you check out. The trend towards unbundling and using fees as a revenue stream means there are many more opportunities to screw things up. Here are some tips:

- Travel with carry-on luggage only if you can. Baggage fees can be eye-watering on budget airlines, so a small bag you can take on board could save you hundreds.
- Think carefully about how much a specific seat is worth to you. On short-haul flights where the difference between seats isn’t huge, leaving the system to allocate one might well save you a lot.
- Be alert for unwanted extras automatically selected for you on OTA pages and in airline booking flows, like travel insurance, “priority customer support,” airport transfers, and other services. Turn these off if you don’t want them.
- Always look at the total price per person, not just the headline fare. Cheap flights become expensive again very quickly if you aren’t careful.

## A consistent search practice

The cheapest way to search for flights isn’t some secret hack, it is simply a matter of adopting a consistent search practice. Follow these rules:

- Book early enough to get within the window where cheap flights can be found.
- Let the price of flights influence the date you leave and, perhaps, your destination, rather than vice versa.
- Use search engines and tools for what they’re good at, meaning wide comparisons, flexible dates, and alerts, but do not conflate search with booking.
- Give priority to transparency, checking out baggage allowance, seat selection fees, and cancellation policy before paying.
- Unless there’s a clear reason why an alternative route is cheaper than a like-for-like option, book directly with the airline.
- Take advantage of AI and automation to reduce the effort you need to put in yourself, but keep checking back on live prices to validate them.
